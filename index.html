Experiment 2: broken glass a week later

Three groups saw a film of a multiple-car accident. One was asked about cars that ‘smashed’ into each other, one about cars that ‘hit’, and a control group was not asked about speed. A week later everyone was asked if they saw any broken glass. There was none.

Chi-squared compares the yes and no counts across the three groups (df = 2). The original counts were 16, 7 and 6 yes answers out of 50 in each group. Check the calculation by hand against the table of critical values in your course materials.

Why does a verb change the answer?

Loftus and Palmer offered two explanations for the result.

Response-bias

The memory is untouched. The verb changes how the witness answers. If you are unsure of the speed, ‘smashed’ suggests a higher number, so you pick one.

Predicts: the effect appears when answering, with no lasting change to memory.

Memory change (substitution)

The information in the question is combined with the original memory, producing a new version. The witness later recalls details that fit the question, not the event.

Predicts: false details appear later, even when the question is no longer asked.

Which result separates them?

Select each observation to see which explanation it favours.

Evaluation points

StrengthControlled laboratory experiment

Only the verb differed between groups, and participants were randomly allocated. This supports a cause-and-effect conclusion: the wording of the question affected the estimate.

Link back: say that the leading question was the cause, not just something that went along with the difference.

StrengthEvidence that memory changed

In Experiment 2, the ‘smashed’ group reported more broken glass a week later than the ‘hit’ and control groups. The false detail lasted beyond the moment of questioning.

StrengthSupported by later research

Loftus and Zanni (1975) showed participants a film of a car accident and asked about a broken headlight. Using ‘the’ (“Did you see the broken headlight?”) produced more yes answers than ‘a’, because ‘the’ presumes the item exists. Even small words can lead.

LimitationLow ecological validity

Students watched filmed accidents in a lab. They had no personal involvement, felt no stress and faced no consequences. Real witnesses may pay closer attention and be harder to mislead.

Yuille and Cutshall (1986) interviewed witnesses to a real shooting in Canada. Their accounts stayed accurate months later and they largely resisted leading questions. This suggests the finding may not generalise to emotional, real events.

LimitationSpeed is hard to judge

People are poor at estimating speed, so participants were uncertain and open to suggestion. Details that a witness is confident about, such as a familiar face, may be harder to alter.

LimitationDemand characteristics and sample

Participants may have treated the verb as a clue to what the experimenter expected. All participants were students, so the sample may not represent older witnesses or other cultures.

ApplicationPolice and courts

The research supports using open, non-leading questions with witnesses, and is one reason interview methods such as the cognitive interview ask witnesses to recall freely. Guidance for interviewing witnesses in England and Wales also favours open questions. Courts can treat leading questions in examination-in-chief with caution.

Exam tip. In a longer evaluation answer, make each point do three jobs: state it, give evidence from the study, then explain what it means for how far leading questions really affect eyewitness testimony.

Spot the leading question

A leading question suggests the answer or assumes a detail that may not exist. Decide which of these an officer should avoid.
